Days 2–3
Sat–Sun, June 13–14 · Family time near Bishop
Family

Two open days in the Owens Valley with family — no fixed schedule. The high Sierra is right there: alpine lakes, ancient bristlecone pines, or just a relaxed valley day.

  • Bishop Creek Canyon & the Alpine Lakes

    Just 20 miles west of Bishop, Highway 168 climbs into a spectacular canyon ending at three crystal-clear alpine lakes between 9,000 and 10,200 feet. The paved road means no serious hiking required — you can drive to the water's edge and picnic with the Sierra Nevada towering on all sides.

    • South Lake — highest and most dramatic; rocky shoreline, stunning Sierra views
    • Lake Sabrina — accessible fishing pier, café with homemade pie and ice cream
    • North Lake — quieter, beautiful aspen groves surrounding the shore
    • Allow 3–4 hours: scenic drive up + picnic + explore the shoreline
    Tips: Bring layers — elevation is 15–20°F cooler than the valley. Kids may feel the altitude (9,000 ft) — slow down, drink extra water. Pack a picnic from Schat's Bakkery or Pupfish Cafe in Bishop. Road is paved but winding — take it easy.

  • Ancient Bristlecone Pine Forest (optional day trip)

    90 minutes from Big Pine in the White Mountains, this forest holds the world's oldest living trees — some over 4,800 years old, older than the Egyptian pyramids. The short Discovery Trail and visitor center make it accessible for young kids, and there's something genuinely mind-bending about standing next to a tree that was already ancient when Rome was founded.

    • Discovery Trail — short, easy loop perfect for kids; gnarled, twisted ancient trees unlike anything else
    • Schulman Grove Visitor Center — exhibits explaining the trees' astonishing age
    • Ranger talks at 11am & 2pm in summer — great for curious kids
    • Sweeping views of the Owens Valley 5,000 feet below
    Tips: 10,000 ft elevation — bring jackets (40–60°F up there), drink extra water, and take it slow. Allow 4–5 hours total including driving. Skip the Methuselah Trail (4.2 mi on exposed ridge — too long for young kids). Visitor center hours ~10am–4:30pm.

  • Late aft.Cathedral Gorge State Park

    Ancient lakebed sediment eroded over millions of years into a surreal landscape of cathedral-like clay spires, slot canyon mazes, and cave-like formations. One of Nevada's most underrated parks — and the narrow crawl-through tunnels make it genuinely thrilling for young kids. (NV State Parks)

    • Slot canyon maze — wander between towering pink and tan clay formations up to 30 feet tall
    • Crawl-through "rabbit hole" tunnels — a highlight kids remember for years; narrow passages that pop you out into open spaces
    • Easy 1-mile loop trails through the canyon — totally manageable for young kids
    • Shaded picnic ramadas at the entrance (perfect for your cooler lunch)
    • Wildlife: lizards sunning on rocks, ravens, occasional raptors overhead
    Tips: $10/vehicle. Flat, easy walking — very accessible for young kids. Allow 1–1.5 hours. Restrooms at the entrance. Late afternoon visit avoids peak heat. Keep kids away from unstable clay walls. Watch for loose soil in the slot sections.

    🗺️
    Tap to load map

  • MiddayZion shuttle + Riverside Walk into the Narrows

    Zion's most iconic family walk: a paved 2.2-mile path following the Virgin River deep into the canyon, where the walls rise 1,000 feet straight overhead. At the trail's end, kids can wade in the cool, crystal-clear river — one of the best hot-day activities in any national park, and genuinely magical.

    • Board the free shuttle at the Visitor Center → ride to Stop 9 (Temple of Sinawava)
    • Walk the flat, paved Riverside Walk (2.2 mi round trip) along the river
    • Reach the Narrows entry and wade in the cool Virgin River (~55°F — refreshingly cold in June heat)
    • You can turn back at any point — no need to commit to the full wading hike
    • Canyon walls provide shade through most of the walk
    Tips: This stop gets packed by 10am — go first thing. Bring water shoes or old sneakers for the river wading. The paved walk is stroller-accessible. The full Narrows wading hike beyond the paved section involves icy water up to your waist and slippery rocks — the paved section gives you the views without the challenge. That's the right call with young kids. (NPS trail info)

  • MiddayValley of Fire State Park

    Nevada's oldest state park, where ancient Jurassic sand dunes turned to vivid crimson sandstone over 150 million years. (NV State Parks) June heat is extreme (110°F+) so stick to the shortest stops — but even a rapid drive-through with two stops delivers some of the most striking red rock scenery in the Southwest.

    • Atlatl Rock — climb a short staircase to a panel of 2,000-year-old Ancestral Puebloan petroglyphs. One of the most accessible rock art sites in Nevada.
    • Elephant Rock — a 0.3-mile loop to a sandstone formation that genuinely looks like an elephant. Quick, flat, and the kids love spotting the shape.
    • Rainbow Vista pullout — no walking required; panoramic views of banded red, orange, white, and purple rock formations.
    • The Beehives (near east entrance) — dome-shaped formations visible from the road; stop for 5 min.
    ⚠ Heat warning: June highs regularly exceed 110°F. All trails over 1 mile are closed May–September. Visit Atlatl Rock and Elephant Rock before 9:30am or after 4:30pm. Bring 2–3 liters of water per person. Reapply sunscreen every 30 min. Visitor Center (9am–4:30pm) has shade, water, and AC.
